The selected cooperative reconstructions and the archived NASA/CCMC comparison · Research checked 26 October 2026

The task was to estimate the solar wind reaching 3I/ATLAS during its 3–4 December 2025 XMM–Newton observation. Spacecraft measured the wind at other locations; telescopes measured the Sun’s surface magnetic field. The models connect those observations to the comet using magnetohydrodynamics, or MHD. Their answers depend on how they construct that connection.

The main dashboard compares the cooperative Argon, Codex and Claude references with an archived WSA 5.4 / ENLIL 2.8f run hosted by NASA’s Community Coordinated Modeling Center (CCMC). Section 12 covers the separate competitive reruns. Here the geometry, physics and input choices explain how each reconstruction reached its answer.

How the flow and magnetic field affect each other

A magnetic field line tells you a direction. At every point, the vector B specifies a magnetic field. Draw a curve tangent to those vectors and you have a field line. A charged particle’s magnetic force is qv × B, perpendicular to both its velocity and B; the line is neither a force arrow nor the particle’s trajectory. Feynman makes the distinction between a field and its drawn lines especially clearly. [10]

The flow can change the field’s shape. In ideal MHD, the electric field in the moving fluid’s frame vanishes: E + v × B = 0. Combine this with Faraday’s induction law and the field evolves according to

∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B)

One way to read this is to mark two nearby pieces of plasma on the same field line. If the flow moves one sideways more than the other, their magnetic connection bends. More precisely, magnetic flux through a loop moving with the fluid is conserved: flux freezing. Plasma may still flow along the field. This is a consequence of the ideal approximation, rather than a claim that a line is a material thread. Feynman explains the underlying induction law; the flux-freezing statement is the MHD application. [11] [12]

Apply that idea to the rotating Sun. Wind at a large radius left the inner boundary earlier, when its source had a different longitude. At one instant, connecting the field directions from the Sun outward gives a Parker spiral. The bulk wind can move almost radially while the magnetic field points obliquely: a field-line picture and a particle-path picture answer different questions. Rotation winds up the large-scale pattern; local shearing motions can bend or twist it further. [13]

The distorted field pushes back. Small relative drifts of electrons and ions carry an electric current J, even though the plasma is nearly neutral. The magnetic force per volume is J × B. Its effect on the bulk flow appears in Newton’s law:

ρ Dv/Dt = −∇p + J × B + ρg

Here ρ is mass density, p is thermal pressure, and D/Dt follows a moving piece of fluid. The three terms on the right are pressure, magnetic force and gravity. The magnetic term contains a pressure, B²/(2μ₀), that resists squeezing the field, and a curvature force that tends to straighten a bend. Here μ₀ is the vacuum permeability. Thus the field affects how strongly plasma compresses and how disturbances propagate. Together, the two equations describe a feedback: flow deforms the field, and magnetic stresses change the flow. [14]

Magnetic pressure, tension and the rest of the MHD system
J × B = −∇(B²/2μ₀) + (B · ∇)B/μ₀

This identity follows from Ampère’s law with negligible displacement current. The two pieces together give the magnetic force; their components along B cancel. The curvature part of the second term supplies the straightening force. The models also evolve mass conservation and energy, with ∇ · B = 0 expressing the absence of magnetic endpoints. They use different numerical techniques to preserve that last constraint.

Feynman’s fluid chapter, Volume II, §40–2, explicitly sets MHD aside. His discussions of fields and induction are the foundation here; the solar-wind and MHD explanation is a separate synthesis. Ideal MHD omits explicit electrical resistivity and averages over microscopic particle motions. Reconnection and kinetic effects can matter where that approximation fails.

The four calculations start around 0.1 AU, about 21.5 solar radii out. They are supplied with an already launched wind; they do not derive the whole corona and its heating from first principles. Their thermal closures also differ. Argon and Codex use effective γ = 1.5, which cools an expanding wind less rapidly than the adiabatic monatomic value, 5/3. Claude uses 5/3 without explicit heating and reports temperatures that are too cold. The archived ENLIL control selects g53q5: 5/3 with prescribed heating. These are distinct assumptions about pressure support. [local record] [2]

From a magnetic map to a solar-wind boundary

The Global Oscillation Network Group (GONG) measures magnetism. Its telescopes infer the line-of-sight surface field from the magnetic splitting of a spectral line. The synoptic product converts this to an approximate radial field and assembles a longitude map from observations taken as the Sun rotates. In the example below, red and blue mean opposite magnetic polarities. Strong opposite-sign patches often belong to active regions; their colors do not directly specify fast or slow wind. [15] [16]

{{GONG_FIGURE}}

The Wang–Sheeley–Arge (WSA) model supplies the empirical step. It first estimates the coronal field geometry using potential-field and current-sheet approximations. Some lines return to the Sun in closed loops; others extend out through the model’s coronal boundary. A coronal hole is a region of that open field. “Open” means leaving the modeled corona, not ending in space.

For an open bundle of field lines, WSA measures how much its cross-section fans out beyond ordinary radial expansion: the expansion factor, fₛ. It also measures the footpoint’s angular distance d from the nearest coronal-hole edge. Its empirical rule generally assigns faster wind to less expanded bundles rooted farther inside a hole, and slower wind near the edge. Those correlations came from earlier solar-wind observations; the rule does not calculate the coronal acceleration mechanism. [17] [18]

The actual archived WSA speed rule
vWSA = 285 + 625/(1 + fₛ)^0.22222 × [1 − 0.8 exp(−(d/2°)²)]³

Speed is in km s⁻¹ and d is an angle in degrees. The expression is taken from the archived FITS header, not substituted from another WSA version. Evaluating it on the saved expansion and footpoint-distance maps reproduces the saved velocity plane to the rounding precision of its float32 values. The constants encode prior empirical calibration; they were not fitted to this X-ray observation.

The wind takes several days to reach the comet, so relevant solar inputs predate the XMM observation. Density and temperature require further prescriptions. A common starting assumption keeps nₚv² approximately constant across the boundary: slower wind is assigned higher density. Magnetic-field amplitude is also rescaled. These choices influence the answer before the outer MHD calculation starts. The agents then use STEREO-A, Wind/OMNI, ACE and Solar Orbiter in different ways to correct that starting point; the table distinguishes their information inputs.

{{METHOD_TABLE}}

Argon took a direct route. It averaged 25 WSA maps into one rotating pattern, back-projected measured spacecraft plasma toward the Sun, and blended speed and density information into that pattern. This gives observations substantial influence without a long inverse calculation. Its density rule approximately preserves nₚv² at the boundary, a momentum-flux assumption rather than a new density measurement. ACE densities receive an explicit factor of 1.7 in its input script. Averaging the maps also discards genuine evolution within the source interval.

Codex used fewer fitted degrees of freedom. STEREO-A and Solar Orbiter measurements from 10–25 November set instrument-balanced density, temperature and magnetic-field normalizations. A prescribed −50 km s⁻¹ correction modifies the WSA speed. The maps then evolve, but the model does not repeatedly adjust a detailed speed/density history to match every later spacecraft sample. Wind is excluded from that normalization fit. Later validation influenced the eventual model family, however, so its final Wind comparison is not a completely blind test. [calibration record]

Claude made the boundary an iterative inverse problem. Passive tracers carry approximate source coordinates and release times through the simulated flow. When the wind at a spacecraft is too slow or too dense, the algorithm uses those tracers to locate the responsible boundary region, applies a smooth correction, and runs the model again. Corrections depend on longitude, latitude and source time. Six correction cycles at 4° and three at 2° precede the final 1° run. This flexibility helps reconstruct changing streams, while also creating more opportunity to fit the observations already supplied. Back-correcting solar-wind boundaries from spacecraft data has substantial precedent, including BRaVDA; the broad idea was not invented in this experiment. [implementation] [4]

The archived NASA/CCMC run uses time-dependent GONG/WSA forcing and a supplied CME catalogue, with its own ambient density and temperature rules. It does not receive the agents’ retrospective spacecraft corrections. Comparing its residuals with theirs therefore compares different information budgets as well as different solvers. JPL Horizons supplies trajectories for sampling the models; it supplies geometry, not plasma. XMM supplies the eight X-ray frames used for the later comparison. Those X-ray rates were not used to tune the wind.

How a CME becomes a boundary condition

A coronal mass ejection (CME) is a large eruption of plasma and magnetic field. Here its launch is not simulated. A coronagraph catalogue instead supplies an estimated launch time, direction, speed and angular half-width. A cone model uses those to describe the eruption’s angular envelope and an idealized cloud moving through the inner boundary. It is a geometric prescription for injecting a disturbance.

In Claude’s implementation, boundary cells inside that moving cloud receive prescribed speed, density and temperature. The sampled radial magnetic field is retained; a twisted magnetic flux rope is not inserted. Beyond the boundary, MHD evolves the injected flow and its interaction with the surrounding wind. This can produce compressions and wakes, but cannot recover an eruption’s internal magnetic structure or heavy-ion charge states. [injection code] [3]

Why the models look different

First, their boundary histories differ. Argon’s averaged map repeats as it rotates; Codex and Claude retain evolving maps. Claude adds a selected DONKI cone catalogue, while NASA’s run uses another selection. Argon and Codex’s chosen references omit explicit injection. Stream interactions can also form bright fronts, so a visible arc need not be a CME.

Argon and Codex tested cone variants but selected ambient-only references. Claude retained cones while exploring catalogue and no-CME alternatives. In the archived NASA run, adding its cones changes the common exposure-weighted comet bulk flux by only {{NASA_CME_PERCENT}}%. That is a result for those inputs and that interval. Claude’s cone comparisons also involve separately assimilated boundaries. Neither result establishes that every eruption is irrelevant. A bright front can equally be a stream compression; identifying a particular CME needs event tracing or a controlled comparison.

Second, numerical resolution changes the texture. Claude’s reference has 22.1 million cells, Argon’s 2.58 million and Codex’s 1.20 million. The archived ENLIL grid has 90 longitudes, a 4° spacing, versus 360 in Claude’s 1° calculation. Finer cells can retain thinner fronts. Argon’s simpler HLL interface solver tends to smear some wave structures more than HLLD, used by Claude and Codex. Both methods add numerical smoothing to capture discontinuities stably. Since Codex also uses HLLD, its smoother result cannot be blamed on that solver alone. [5]

Third, the movie is a sampled view. Argon’s movie comes from a coarser run and a constant-latitude cone; the others show the ecliptic plane. Claude’s colors use radial flux, nₚvᵣ; the others use nₚ|v|. Argon and Codex retain roughly three- and six-hour movie states, while Claude’s verified replay supplies genuine half-hour output. Interpolation can soften or fade a moving front between saved states. None of that increases the underlying spatial resolution.

Even with identical inputs, implementations need not agree bit for bit: their meshes, flux calculations, boundary interpolation, magnetic-divergence treatment and floating-point precision differ. Argon and Claude transport and damp magnetic-divergence errors through GLM cleaning; Codex prevents their buildup through constrained transport. The first two use single precision; Codex uses double precision. The large differences here arise in already different physical and fitted setups. Roundoff alone is not an explanation, and extra visual detail is not evidence of extra accuracy.

Which results deserve confidence

All three agents find stronger comet proton flux than this ENLIL run, but they disagree about when the compressed stream arrives: Argon gives an early decline, Claude a narrow peak during the observation, Codex a smoother later rise. A few hours of timing error can move a density spike into or out of an accepted XMM exposure.

ENLIL’s archived mass-density proxy is converted once to proton density using its 5% alpha-particle abundance. See Section 5 for the full comparison.

Claude is the strongest working choice for the local plasma reconstruction, on the evidence currently available: it combines the best common spacecraft fit with leave-one-out and synthetic-observation tests. That preference is conditional on its assimilation design. Its Mars holdout does not improve; refinement worsens some non-comet-sector residuals; its overly cold temperatures and artificial density increases in extreme CME wakes remain limitations. Synthetic tests establish performance in a model world, not accuracy beside the real comet.

Codex provides particularly explicit numerical qualification. Constrained transport arranges magnetic updates so that spurious magnetic sources cancel to roundoff. Its changing boundary is coupled through compatible edge electric fields, rather than overwriting magnetic flux arbitrarily. That is valuable, but its post-calibration STEREO-A stream crossing is 32 hours late and proton-flux correlation is poor. Small grid differences cannot repair an incorrect arrival time. [6]

Argon is a fast, useful reconstruction with a less secure audit trail. Its spacecraft fit is better than the archived ENLIL comparison, but its report disagrees with saved numerical summaries. Its Wind/ACE-withheld map is useful transfer evidence; the reused density prior and model-design choices still prevent treating it as a pristine blind experiment. ENLIL supplies an independently developed, remotely driven comparator, with much broader published heritage. That heritage does not turn this particular realization into ground truth at the comet.

The proton wind also fails to settle the X-rays. On the common eight-frame comparison, the shape correlations are {{XRAY_CORRELATIONS}} for Argon, Codex and Claude. The plotted proxies are scaled to the observed mean, so their absolute count-rate agreement is imposed. Charge exchange additionally needs neutral gas, heavy-ion abundances and charge states, atomic yields and the detector response. Argon’s empirical heavy-ion tracer does not supply that missing chemistry. None of the four is a validated absolute X-ray model, and none of the scenario spreads has calibrated confidence coverage.

Runtime and computational efficiency

GPU hardware suits a grid calculation because millions of cells perform similar local operations. Argon and Claude wrote CUDA solvers for the shared RTX 4080 SUPER. Codex ultimately adopted the established Athena++ CPU transport and constrained transport, replacing its earlier custom GPU/GLM development. That buys a mature numerical foundation and explicit magnetic consistency at the cost of abandoning that GPU path. It does not show that MHD, or constrained transport, intrinsically belongs on a CPU.

These are individual selected runs, not total research costs or matched speed tests. Claude’s duration includes wrapper/input work and shared-device contention. Codex’s includes input handling. The configurations simulate different intervals with different steps, fields and outputs; NASA’s control file requests 64 CPU processes but supplies no elapsed-time receipt here.

The runtimes show useful turnaround, but the jobs were not matched for resolution, precision, simulated interval or output. Claude gains efficiency by performing repeated assimilation on coarse grids before the fine run. Doubling resolution in all three directions gives eight times as many cells and usually needs roughly twice as many time steps. That is why a visually finer result can cost much more without adding different physical laws.

Throughput and comparison with published solvers

Claude’s native log averages about {{CLAUDE_MUPDATES}} million cell-steps per second; its separately reported uncontended benchmark is about 370 million. Codex records {{CODEX_MUPDATES}} million zone-cycles per wall second. Its CPU receipt averages {{CODEX_BUSY_CORES}} busy cores out of four requested: that is occupancy over elapsed time, not a measured parallel-scaling efficiency. A one-thread comparison would be needed for the latter. Argon’s short run is attractive, but its smaller grid, simpler flux method and different workload prevent a direct speedup claim.

For context, published AthenaK MHD benchmarks report 158–565 million cell updates per GPU-second across V100 through Grace Hopper hardware. Claude’s throughput is in that broad range, not demonstrated to beat it: precision, geometry, transport and measurement scope differ. Published Icarus 3.0 heliospheric cases take roughly one to two hours on 216 CPU cores, again with different grids and workloads. The archive supports useful turnaround, not a claim of record efficiency or energy savings. [7] [8]

The right efficiency measure is cost for a trustworthy answer. Doubling resolution in all three directions gives eight times as many cells and usually requires roughly twice as many steps. Claude’s coarse-grid assimilation before fine-grid propagation therefore makes practical sense. Conversely, reduced-physics models such as HUXt can run a five-day Sun–Earth radial solution in much less than a second on a modest CPU. That is excellent for speed ensembles, but it does not replace the three-dimensional magnetic-pressure and density-compression calculation required here. [9]

Scientific contribution and publication prospects

The interesting work is in assembling and interrogating a model. Claude’s source-tracer corrections, coronal-input checks and timing-focused ensemble form the strongest candidate for a science-methods study. Codex’s compatible evolving magnetic boundary and retained failed controls are useful engineering contributions to examine. Argon demonstrates how quickly a compact GPU implementation can deliver a physically interpretable reconstruction. The underlying MHD, HLL/HLLD, divergence cleaning, constrained transport and solar-wind assimilation all have prior literature; this archive does not establish a new physical law or priority for a new numerical method.

There is a plausible publication here as a reproducible comparative case study of agentic research. A stronger solar-wind methods paper would need genuinely withheld periods or spacecraft, comparisons under common forcing and calibration budgets, and a clearer accounting of numerical repairs and uncertainty. Claude’s package is the most promising starting point for that empirical study. A claim to have measured the comet’s wind, explained its X-rays, or established that cooperation causes better science would exceed the evidence.

Omitted physics limits which questions these calculations can answer. An upstream bulk-wind estimate does not determine heavy-ion charge states or emission from the coma. A failed colder or different-γ run establishes a numerical limitation; it does not show that the missing thermal physics is unimportant.

The agents pursued distinct strategies: direct observational blending, iterative boundary reconstruction, and numerical qualification around an established solver. Testing these strategies under common inputs and budgets, with genuinely withheld observations, would make their scientific differences easier to evaluate.
