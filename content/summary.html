Three cooperative reference models and one archived NASA/CCMC run · Sources checked 26 October 2026

On 3–4 December 2025, XMM–Newton recorded X-rays from the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS. Comets emit X-rays when heavy ions in the solar wind capture electrons from the comet’s gas, so to interpret the observation we need to know what wind was hitting the comet at the time. No spacecraft was there to measure it. What we do have are spacecraft measurements of the wind elsewhere, mostly near 1 AU, and telescope maps of the magnetic field on the Sun’s surface. The models described here use magnetohydrodynamics, or MHD, to work out what those measurements imply for the wind at the comet, 1.87 AU from the Sun.

Three AI agents, Argon, Codex and Claude, worked cooperatively and shared notes. Each built such a model, tried many variations, and picked one run as its best estimate, which we call the reference run. (Section 12 covers a separate set of competitive reruns.) We compare those runs with one from ENLIL, the standard MHD model of the heliosphere used in space-weather work, archived by NASA’s Community Coordinated Modeling Center (CCMC). [1] Heavy ions make up only a few parts in ten thousand of the wind, and their abundance at the comet was not measured, so we follow the protons and take the heavy-ion flux to be a small fraction of the proton flux. The quantity we care about is therefore the proton flux at the comet: the number of protons crossing each square centimeter per second, or density times speed (nₚ|v|). All four models start from the same solar maps, yet their estimates run from 0.44 × 10⁸ cm⁻² s⁻¹ for ENLIL to 2.4 × 10⁸ for Argon, more than a factor of five apart.

Each parcel of wind flows almost straight out from the Sun, but the pattern of fast and slow wind turns with the Sun at nearly constant latitude. Along any radial line, then, fast wind can leave after slow wind, catch up with it, and compress it. The result is called a stream interaction region, and we will call its dense compressed edge the front. Its signature is dense gas followed by fast wind. STEREO-A saw exactly that on 28 November: the density reached 46 cm⁻³, and within a day the speed rose from about 400 to more than 650 km s⁻¹. STEREO-A was the most useful spacecraft because it sat within about 1° of the comet’s solar latitude and 25° away in longitude, on the side that the Sun’s rotation, counterclockwise in Figure 13.1, sweeps past first. The streams it saw reached the comet several days later. Every model has to work out what that front looked like after another 0.9 AU of travel, and when it arrived.

The wind and the magnetic field

The solar wind is a plasma: its hydrogen and helium are ionized, and it conducts electricity very well. A conductor moving through a magnetic field has currents induced in it. Those currents feel a magnetic force and create field of their own, so the flow and the field have to be solved together. MHD does this by treating the plasma as a single conducting fluid.

The magnetic field B is a vector defined at every point in space. A field line is a curve drawn to run parallel to B at each point along it, with neighboring lines crowding together where the field is strong. Feynman called field lines “only a crude way of describing a field,” [10] and they do invite one mistake: you might read a line as the direction in which a particle is pushed. The force on a charge q moving at velocity v is qv × B, which is perpendicular to both v and B, so a charged particle feels no push along a field line. It spirals around the line while sliding along it. In a conducting fluid the lines are a better guide than Feynman’s remark suggests, because the plasma and the lines move together.

To see why, note that a parcel of plasma cannot sustain an electric field in its own rest frame: any such field would immediately drive currents that cancel it. In the Sun-centered frame of the model, where the parcel moves at velocity v, this condition reads E + v × B = 0. Substituting it into Faraday’s law gives the induction equation:

∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B)

According to this equation, the field at a fixed point changes only because the moving plasma carries, stretches or compresses it. One can show from it that the magnetic flux through any loop moving with the plasma stays constant, a result called flux freezing. Now draw a thin tube around a field line. No flux passes through the side of the tube, and that stays true as the side moves with the plasma, so the tube stays a tube of field lines. Two parcels that start on the same field line therefore stay on the same field line. If the flow carries one of them sideways relative to the other, the line has to bend to keep connecting them. If the flow turns one end of a bundle of lines, the bundle twists. Flux freezing is exact only for a perfect conductor, and in very thin layers real field lines can break and reconnect, but the large-scale solar wind follows it closely. [11] [12]

The simplest example of flux freezing is the Parker spiral. The wind flows out almost radially, while the base of each field line goes around with the Sun once every 25 days or so. A parcel far from the Sun today left days ago from a spot that has since rotated away, and flux freezing keeps it on the field line rooted at that spot. At any instant the line therefore runs through all the parcels released from that spot at successive times, and those parcels form a spiral. A rotating garden sprinkler makes the same shape: each drop flies straight out, but the stream as a whole curves. The spiral makes an angle of about 45° with the radial direction near Earth and about 60° at the comet’s distance, where the wind and the field point in quite different directions (see Figure 13.3 below). [13]

The field pushes back on the flow through the force on the plasma’s currents. The plasma stays almost exactly neutral, but its electrons and ions drift slightly relative to each other, and that drift is a current J. The force per unit volume on this current, J × B, joins pressure and gravity in Newton’s second law for the fluid:

ρ Dv/Dt = −∇p + J × B + ρg

Here ρ is the mass density, p the thermal pressure, g the Sun’s gravity, and D/Dt the rate of change following a moving parcel. The magnetic force can be split into two parts. One is a magnetic pressure, B²/(2μ₀), which resists compression just as gas pressure does (μ₀ is the vacuum permeability). The other is a tension along the field lines, which pulls a bent line straight like a stretched rubber band. Magnetic pressure limits how dense the fronts become: where a fast stream compresses slower wind, the field is compressed with it, and the rising magnetic pressure slows the pile-up. When Argon turned the field off in a test run, the peak density at the comet doubled and the average proton flux over the observation rose 75%. Codex’s version of the test raised its peak by a third but its average flux by only 5%, because its front arrives mostly after the observation. [14]

Magnetic pressure, tension and the rest of the MHD system
J × B = −∇(B²/2μ₀) + (B · ∇)B/μ₀

This identity follows from Ampère’s law when the displacement current is negligible, as it is in the solar wind. The first term is minus the gradient of the magnetic pressure. The part of the second term that points toward the center of curvature of a field line is the tension. Each term also has a component along B, and these two components cancel, since J × B has none. The full set of MHD equations also conserves mass and energy and keeps ∇ · B = 0, which says that field lines have no beginning or end. Ideal MHD ignores electrical resistance and averages over the motions of individual particles. Reconnection and kinetic effects are not in these models.

The equations above don’t say how the wind’s temperature changes as it expands. A monatomic gas expanding with no heat input follows p ∝ ρ^γ with γ = 5/3, so its temperature goes as ρ^(γ−1) and falls quickly as the gas thins out. The real wind cools more slowly because it keeps being heated on the way out. Argon and Codex imitate this heating with a smaller effective γ of 1.5. ENLIL uses 5/3 and adds a prescribed heating term. [control file] [2] Claude uses 5/3 with no heating, and its protons end up about three times too cold. In the tests that finished, changing γ moved the proton flux at the comet by only 1 to 12%. Codex’s final γ = 5/3 runs crashed before reaching the XMM observation.

The four calculations begin on a sphere 0.1 AU from the Sun, or 21.5 solar radii, where the wind is already moving at hundreds of kilometers per second. By starting there, the modelers avoid the hardest questions: how the corona, the Sun’s hot outer atmosphere, is heated, and how the wind is accelerated. A disturbance can travel back toward the Sun only if it moves through the plasma faster than the plasma flows out. At 0.1 AU the wind is far faster than sound and about as fast as the quickest magnetic waves, so very little of what happens farther out can get back to the sphere. Each model therefore has to be told what the wind looks like there: its speed, density, temperature and magnetic field at every latitude, longitude and time. Given this set of maps, called the model’s boundary condition, the MHD equations determine everything farther out. The models solve them on a grid of cells, and we label each grid by its cell size: a 4° grid has cells about 4° across.

From a surface map to the wind at 0.1 AU

The maps start from the magnetic field at the Sun’s surface. A magnetic field splits certain spectral lines into several components (the Zeeman effect), and telescopes of the Global Oscillation Network Group (GONG) use that splitting to measure the field along the line of sight. As the Sun rotates, these observations are combined into a single map of the whole surface and converted to an approximate radial field. The top panel of Figure 13.2 shows the map from 25 November, with red for field pointing out of the Sun and blue for field pointing in. The strong, closely paired patches of opposite sign are mostly active regions, where sunspots form. The solar wind takes about a week to get from 0.1 AU to the comet, so late-November maps like this one are the ones that matter for the XMM observation. [15] [16]

{{GONG_FIGURE}}

To get from this map to a wind speed we use the Wang–Sheeley–Arge (WSA) model, which works in two stages. The first calculates the shape of the magnetic field in the corona. Up to about 2.5 solar radii it assumes that the corona carries no electric currents, in which case the surface map fixes the field everywhere (a potential-field model). Beyond that, the field is taken to be dragged open into nearly radial lines, with outward field on one side of a thin sheet of electric current and inward field on the other. Some field lines in the result loop back to the Sun, while others reach the top of the model and continue into interplanetary space. These are the open field lines. The regions of the surface where they are rooted are called coronal holes.

The second stage assigns a speed to each open field line. The expansion factor fₛ says how much a bundle of field lines fans out through the corona: fₛ = 30 means that its cross-section grows thirty times more than it would in purely radial expansion. The distance d is the angle between the bundle’s footpoint and the nearest coronal-hole boundary. Wang and Sheeley found in 1990 that the wind near Earth is slower where the expansion factor is larger, and later versions of the model added d. Fast wind goes to bundles that fan out little and start deep inside a coronal hole, and slow wind to bundles that fan out widely or start near a hole’s edge. {{WSA_EXAMPLES}}. This stage is an empirical fit to earlier spacecraft observations, with no physics of the wind’s acceleration in it. [17] [18]

The exact WSA speed rule used here
vWSA = 285 + 625/(1 + fₛ)^0.22222 × [1 − 0.8 exp(−(d/2°)²)]³

Speed is in km s⁻¹ and d is in degrees. This is the formula recorded in the header of the WSA maps used here.

WSA’s speed and radial field both need adjusting. Its speed rule was fitted to wind near 1 AU, whereas at 0.1 AU the wind is still accelerating, so the speed is lowered by 25 to 75 km s⁻¹. Most of the models also cap it near 700 km s⁻¹. Models of this kind are known to underestimate the Sun’s open magnetic flux, so the agents multiply the WSA field by a constant: 2.4 in Codex, 3.5 in Argon and 4 in Claude. (ENLIL takes only the polarity from WSA and gives every boundary cell the same field strength.) Density and temperature have to be assumed. The usual assumption is that the momentum flux, which goes as nₚv², is the same everywhere on the sphere, as spacecraft broadly find in the wind. Slow wind then gets a high density and fast wind a low one. Thermal pressure is taken to be uniform as well, so the thin, fast wind comes out hotter.

Codex tested how much these choices matter on a coarse grid. Doubling the boundary temperature raised the proton flux at the comet by about 54%, and raising the density by 36% raised it by 37%. Adding 40 km s⁻¹ to every speed raised it by 31%, far more than the 8% change in speed, probably because a faster wind also changes when the front reaches the comet.

CMEs in the models

A coronal mass ejection (CME) is a large eruption of plasma and magnetic field from the Sun. The models do not simulate the eruption itself. Analysts estimate each CME’s launch time, direction, speed and angular half-width from coronagraph images, in which the Sun’s bright disk is blocked out, and NASA publishes the results in its DONKI catalogue. A cone model turns those numbers into a simple shape: a cloud moving outward along the CME’s direction, with its size set by the half-width.

In Claude’s version, as the cloud crosses the 0.1 AU sphere, the boundary cells inside it take on the cloud’s speed, density and temperature while the radial magnetic field stays as it was. The cloud has none of the twisted magnetic flux rope that a real CME often carries. Beyond the boundary, the MHD equations follow the cloud as it plows through the surrounding wind, producing compressed fronts and low-density wakes. The ENLIL run injects a different selection of DONKI cones. Argon and Codex tried cones in test runs but left them out of their reference runs. [injection code] [3]

Spacecraft data in each model

So far the pattern of streams on the boundary comes entirely from solar observations. Each agent then used spacecraft measurements of the wind to correct its boundary, some far more heavily than others. The agents differ most in this step, which is called data assimilation. The spacecraft are STEREO-A, Solar Orbiter, and NASA’s ACE and Wind spacecraft near Earth.

{{METHOD_TABLE}}
The numerical methods in this table

Each code divides space into small cells and, at every time step, works out how much mass, momentum, energy and magnetic flux pass through each cell’s faces. HLL and HLLD are Riemann solvers, routines that estimate that exchange where neighboring cells disagree. HLLD is the more detailed of the two and smears waves less. [5] GLM and CT keep ∇ · B = 0, which numerical errors would otherwise violate, as if magnetic charges had appeared. GLM (generalized Lagrange multiplier) cleaning sends those errors away as waves and damps them. In CT (constrained transport), the electric field on each cell edge enters the two faces sharing that edge with opposite signs, so the net magnetic flux out of a cell cannot change.

Argon averaged the 25 daily WSA maps, weighted toward late November, into a single map that rotates with the Sun and otherwise stays fixed. It then traced each spacecraft measurement straight back to 0.1 AU at the measured speed, to find where and when that wind left the boundary, and blended the measured speeds and densities into the map. Where coverage was good, the spacecraft values counted for up to 85% of the blend. All of this takes one pass through the data and no repeated simulations. But a single fixed map can’t follow changes on the Sun during those weeks, and the straight-line trace ignores the way streams interact on their way out.

Codex used the spacecraft data only to set three overall scales. From STEREO-A and Solar Orbiter data for 10–25 November, counting both spacecraft equally, it derived one density scale, one temperature scale and one magnetic-field multiplier for the whole boundary. It also lowered every WSA speed by a fixed 50 km s⁻¹. Otherwise the boundary follows the daily WSA maps as they change, so streams stay where WSA puts them, right or wrong. Data from the Wind spacecraft were left out of the fit to serve as a check. Codex did look at later comparisons when it chose its family of models, so its final Wind comparison is not a completely blind test. [Codex calibration file]

Claude treated the boundary as an inverse problem: given what the spacecraft measured, what boundary would have produced it? Its simulation carries passive tracers, labels that move with the fluid without affecting it and record roughly where and when each parcel of wind left the boundary. When the model wind at a spacecraft comes out too slow or too dense, the labels point to the patch of boundary that wind came from. Claude corrects that patch with a smooth adjustment and runs the simulation again. It did this six times at 4° resolution and three more times at 2°, before one final run at 1°. Each daily map gets its own correction in longitude, latitude and time, which lets the model follow streams that change from day to day. It also gives Claude far more adjustable numbers than Codex, so part of its good fit to those spacecraft is built in. Correcting a solar-wind boundary from spacecraft data is an established technique; the BRaVDA scheme of Lang and Owens is a well-known example. [Claude’s code] [4]

Why the models disagree

RMSE is the root-mean-square difference between model and measurement, here over 385 hourly STEREO-A speeds from 19 November to 5 December 2025. Argon and Claude fitted these measurements, Codex fitted no speeds, and ENLIL used no wind measurements. XMM’s EPIC-pn camera recorded usable data only in short intervals, which we call the exposures, and the flux is averaged over them. The comet was moving outward at about 43 km s⁻¹, so the flux that actually struck it was about 8% lower. All four values use one clock and the exact exposure times, so they can differ a little from the agents’ own reports: Claude’s gives 1.6 × 10⁸, for example. ENLIL’s archived density includes helium, and we divide it by 1.2 to get protons. For Argon we use its saved data, which in places disagree with its written report. Section 5 has the details.

ENLIL’s flux is low mainly because its density is low. Over the XMM exposures ENLIL has about 1.1 protons per cubic centimeter moving at 409 km s⁻¹, while the agents have 2.7 to 4.3 at 480 to 590 km s⁻¹. ENLIL’s default boundary density is 125 cm⁻³ for wind at 700 km s⁻¹, which for these weeks was too low: it gives 0.3 to 0.7 of the density measured at STEREO-A, Wind and Solar Orbiter. The agents scaled their density to those measurements, ending up with roughly twice ENLIL’s density at the boundary. Argon’s test runs, on a coarser grid than its reference, show the steps. With ENLIL’s default density and roughly its other settings, the flux was 0.42 × 10⁸, close to ENLIL’s only because two differences cancelled: density about 20% lower and speed about 17% higher. Raising the density by a factor of 1.7 to match the 1 AU measurements, with smaller changes to speed, field and temperature, gave 0.88 × 10⁸. Blending in the spacecraft data gave 2.3 × 10⁸, because the map now included the stream that STEREO-A had measured.

Among the agents, probably most of the difference comes from when the front reaches the comet relative to the XMM exposures. Argon has it arriving early and then declining, Claude has a narrow peak during the observation, and Codex has a smoother, later rise. Suppose the peak moves by six hours. It can then fall in a gap between exposures, and the average changes even though the wind itself barely has. When Claude varied the assumptions in twelve 4° versions of its assimilation and carried the differences over to its 1° run, the front moved from 7 hours earlier to 20 hours later, and the estimated proton flux ranged from 0.5 to 3.1 × 10⁸.

Averaged over the XMM exposures, the cones changed the proton flux very little, mainly because they had already passed the comet. In Argon’s test they changed the flux there by up to 49% on 27 November, but by less than 0.1% from 2 December on. ENLIL’s cones changed its average by {{NASA_CME_PERCENT}}%, but its list omitted the two CMEs of 23 November that were aimed most directly at the comet. Argon’s 16 cones include both, though about a day early. A separate Codex test of the pair, at the catalogued times, changed its average by less than 1%. Their true speeds are uncertain, and a slower arrival on 3–4 December was not tested. Claude’s variant with no CMEs gives a flux about 15% higher, but because it had its own separate assimilation on a coarser grid, the difference can’t be pinned on the CMEs alone.

Even with identical inputs, two different codes would not agree bit for bit, because their grids, numerical precision and flux calculations differ. Since no two of these runs differ in only one respect, we can’t say exactly how much of the spread comes from numerics, but Codex’s early coarse-grid tests give a rough idea. Changing only the routine that computes the flow between cells, from the simpler HLLE to HLLD, lowered the proton flux at the comet by about 15%. Changing how it cleaned ∇ · B, along with the numerical dissipation tied to it, raised the flux by about 25%. So numerical choices can probably change the flux by a few tens of percent, which is small next to the factor of five between Argon and ENLIL but as large as the 18% between Codex and Claude.

Differences between the movies

The movies on the dashboard look very different, with sharp arcs in some and smooth bands in others. Their boundaries differ: Argon’s single map repeats exactly as it rotates, Codex and Claude use maps that change from day to day, and only the Claude and ENLIL runs inject CMEs. A bright arc can be a CME or a stream front, because a fast stream running into slower wind looks much the same. To identify a particular front you have to trace it back or compare runs with and without the CME.

Grid size matters as well, because a front spreads over at least a few cells. Claude’s 1° grid has 360 cells in longitude against ENLIL’s 90 (4° spacing), while in the radial direction the two have about the same spacing, 0.005 AU. Argon’s HLL solver also smears some waves more than the HLLD solver used by Claude and Codex. Codex’s movie is smooth all the same, and its grid can’t be the reason, because its cells are about the same size as those in Argon’s movie run. The likelier causes are its boundary, which has no CMEs or local spacecraft corrections to add fine structure, and its widely spaced frames: interpolating between saved frames softens a moving front, most of all when they are six hours apart.

Which results to trust

We can test a fitted model on data left out of its fit, and the agents did this in different ways. When Claude withheld one spacecraft at a time in 4° tests, the speed error there was still 15 to 24% lower than in the uncorrected model. At Mars, whose MAVEN orbiter was never used in any fit, its corrections brought no improvement: the speed error stays near 120 km s⁻¹. Argon’s result is the least encouraging. With Wind and ACE withheld, its speed error at Wind was 95 km s⁻¹, worse than the 64 km s⁻¹ of its ENLIL-like first run, and the median flux there matched only with a default density already set using Wind data. Codex’s speed error at Wind, which it did not fit, is 123 km s⁻¹, a little better than ENLIL’s 142. ENLIL used no wind measurements at all, so in these weeks the solar maps alone predicted the wind poorly.

None of the models can place the front in time as well as we would like. Codex’s numerics are the cleanest of the three, yet even after calibration its fast stream reaches STEREO-A 32 hours late, and the proton flux there is essentially uncorrelated with the measurements (r = −0.09). [6] ENLIL’s stream is about two days late at STEREO-A as well. That points to a fault in the WSA maps, which a finer grid would not help. Claude’s fit to STEREO-A is much better, at 32 km s⁻¹. But the front still has another 0.9 AU to travel, and by Claude’s own report the method cannot place it at the comet to better than about a day.

Behind Claude’s fastest cones the density falls so low that magnetic waves become extremely fast and the time step collapses, so the code adds artificial density. This happens in about one cell update in ten thousand and leaves no sign at the comet. More seriously, a 4° version with no cones fits every spacecraft better, including Mars (72 against 126 km s⁻¹). The cones represent real events, and DONKI links the 23 November pair to a shock seen at STEREO-A on 26 November, but this result suggests they may be doing harm elsewhere. Claude’s corrections were also tuned at 2°, and using them at 1° worsened the fit near Earth and at Solar Orbiter.

We take Claude’s run as our best estimate of the proton flux averaged over the observation, about 1.5 × 10⁸ cm⁻² s⁻¹. It fits STEREO-A, the most relevant spacecraft, better than any other run, although part of that fit is built in, and its boundary can change from day to day. The range is wide: the middle two-thirds of its twelve versions lie between about 0.9 and 2.2 × 10⁸. Because the front’s arrival is uncertain by about a day, the hour-by-hour shape of the curve can’t be trusted, and the average itself depends on where the peak falls among the exposures.

Claude also tested a simpler version of its method on synthetic data, in which a known model wind is hidden and has to be recovered from simulated spacecraft measurements. This version ran at 4° with one correction map that does not change in time, and the hidden errors were steady too. Only one of the three trials started far from the right answer: its flux error at the comet fell from +178% to +14%, or only to +53% without STEREO-A. The other two started within 2% and ended within 9%. The time-varying version used for the reference never finished this test. These tests also assume that the model’s physics is right, so the real error is probably larger.

Proton flux alone does not reproduce the X-ray light curve, and none of the models used the X-ray data to tune the wind. The count rate fell by a factor of about eight in the first six hours of the observation, then climbed almost all the way back. Over the eight time bins of the light curve, the correlations between flux and count rate are {{XRAY_CORRELATIONS}} for Argon, Codex and Claude. With only eight points the first two can’t be told from zero. Claude’s is negative because its front falls close to the X-ray minimum, but with its timing uncertain by about a day, even that sign means little. ENLIL’s is +0.41, on a flux that barely changes over these bins, from 4.1 to 5.3 × 10⁷. Predicting the brightness itself would take the neutral gas around the comet, the charge states of the heavy ions, the atomic charge-exchange yields and the detector response, all of which lie outside these models.

Computing cost

A grid-based MHD code suits a GPU, because millions of cells all do the same local arithmetic and can be updated in parallel. Argon and Claude each wrote a solver in CUDA, NVIDIA’s GPU language, for a shared RTX 4080 SUPER. Codex also started with its own code, first on the CPU and then ported to the GPU. Its results depended strongly on how ∇ · B was cleaned, and rarefied cells became implausibly cold, so it switched to Athena++, an established CPU code whose constrained transport holds ∇ · B at the level of roundoff error even as the boundary field changes. Its runs are slower without the GPU.

Doubling the resolution in all three directions gives eight times as many cells. It also usually takes about twice as many time steps, because the largest stable time step shrinks with the cell size. The work goes up roughly sixteen times, which is why Claude did its repeated correction runs on coarse grids and kept the 1° grid for one final run.

Throughput and comparison with published solvers

Claude’s solver averaged {{CLAUDE_MUPDATES}} million cell updates per second on the shared GPU, and about 370 million with the GPU to itself. Codex’s averaged {{CODEX_MUPDATES}} million per second on four threads, which kept {{CODEX_BUSY_CORES}} cores busy on average. There is no one-thread run to compare with, so we can’t say how well those threads scaled.

Published AthenaK MHD benchmarks report 158–565 million cell updates per second per device, from the NVIDIA V100 up to Grace Hopper. Claude’s speed is in that range, but AthenaK’s numbers come from a simple wave test on a uniform grid, which makes the comparison rough. Published Icarus 3.0 heliospheric runs take about one to two hours on six 36-core nodes. At the other extreme, reduced-physics models such as HUXt solve a five-day Sun–Earth radial wind in much less than a second on a modest CPU. HUXt neglects magnetic and pressure forces and follows only the wind speed, so it can’t give the density of the front. [7] [8] [9]

What could be published

All the numerical methods here are established: HLL and HLLD solvers, divergence cleaning, constrained transport and solar-wind data assimilation all have long histories. The closest thing to new work is Claude’s use of passive tracers to trace spacecraft errors back to a boundary that changes with time. Together with its twelve-run test of how far the front’s arrival time can move, that could become a methods paper. Codex’s way of driving a changing magnetic boundary through edge electric fields may also interest modelers, since it lets the boundary field change daily without creating magnetic charge.

The most plausible paper is a reproducible case study of how AI agents do this kind of research, with the runs, notes and failed tests all saved. A solar-wind methods paper would need more: time periods or spacecraft withheld from every model, the same inputs for all of them, and error bars shown to contain the true value about as often as they claim. A paper of either kind could report the flux at the comet with its wide spread, but it could not claim to explain the X-rays. The comparison in Section 12 is also too small to show whether cooperation produces better science.
