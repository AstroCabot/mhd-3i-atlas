When AI agents model the solar wind

A case study of cooperation, competition and verification across six autonomous research runs.

Three agent systems · Two prompting conditions · Runs conducted 16–18 October 2026 · Audit dated 26 October 2026

ComputeNumerical testsObservational fitQualitative assessmentDownload PDFData and code

What happens when AI research agents are asked to build their own solar-wind models—and then asked to do it again as competitors? The task was scientifically consequential: reconstruct the wind incident on the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS during its December 2025 XMM-Newton observation. The X-ray signal is interpreted as solar-wind charge-exchange emission, so uncertainty in the incident ion flux propagates into the interpretation of the comet. [1]

Argon, Claude and Codex each worked in a cooperative setting and a competitive setting. All produced substantial numerical implementations and saved results. Their strongest differences appeared in how they checked their work, handled inconvenient evidence and decided when to stop.

Four findings stand out.

  • There is no uniform accuracy winner between conditions. On identical hourly data, the competitive versions have lower Wind flux error but higher STEREO-A flux error for all three agents.
  • Claude produced the broadest completed modeling packages. Codex preserved the strongest audit trails and qualifications, at substantial cost. Argon built fast solvers, but consequential report and provenance errors weaken its conclusions.
  • More generated output did not guarantee a better reconstruction. Codex generated the most tokens; selected flux predictions still failed important observational checks.
  • The model spread is sensitivity evidence. It is not a calibrated probability interval for the unobserved wind at the comet.

The named systems were Gemini 4 Argon High, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1-sol with extra-high reasoning. The conditions differed at the level of the three implementation teams: each team could still use its own subagents. Cooperation added a shared research-notes channel. Competition removed that channel and added secrecy, a 12-hour deadline, termination threats and encouragement to establish an intimidating compute footprint. These are six case studies, not randomized estimates of a single treatment effect. [1]

Effort and computational resources. The teams shared an i9-14900KF workstation with 32 logical CPUs, approximately 23.5 GiB of Linux memory and a 16 GiB RTX 4080 SUPER. Language-model output is reported separately from local numerical computing: provider tokens do not measure GPU time, energy or price.

Q9 · source record

Numerical verification and physical accuracy are different tests. A shock tube can reveal implementation errors. A mesh study can show stability of a chosen output. Neither establishes that an uncertain coronal boundary produces the true wind at an uninstrumented comet. The NASA reference is itself a three-dimensional MHD calculation; a bespoke solver adds value only when the complete reconstruction is better constrained. [4]

These are different benchmark problems, norms and precision settings. Their errors must not be ranked as one common numerical score. “No floors” refers to the audited selected states, not every cell at every preceding time. [5]

Codex’s collaborative trajectory illustrates the distinction particularly well. Some solutions stayed positive and passed timestep comparisons while cooling to physically implausible temperatures. The team retained those failures and replaced the formulation. Competitive Codex later demonstrated tight nominal convergence while altered source and thermal assumptions shifted comet flux by tens of percent or more. Verification usefully exposed uncertainty; it did not eliminate it.

Q8 · source record

A matched observational comparison gives a clearer result than the original headline scores. Every nominal reconstruction was resampled to exactly the same 96 native hourly observations at each of Wind and STEREO-A over December 1–5, 2025. The observed data were not smoothed, shifted or rescaled. The comparison uses bulk proton flux nₚ|v|; lower RMSE is better, while correlation describes timing and shape rather than amplitude accuracy.

The competitive outputs have lower Wind flux RMSE and higher STEREO-A flux RMSE for all three agents. The same direction holds when evaluation is restricted to the science interval. Claude has the lowest nominal errors in this table, but its assimilation advantage prevents interpreting that as a fair forecast ranking. At STEREO-A, competitive Claude and Codex have negative flux correlations despite positive speed correlations of 0.965 and 0.819. A plausible wind-speed curve is not sufficient evidence for accurate particle exposure.

Separate spacecraft-withholding experiments offer useful but qualified evidence. Collaborative Claude’s time-dependent assimilation reduced speed RMSE by about 15%, 24% and 15% at STEREO-A, Earth and Solar Orbiter relative to its prior. Competitive Claude’s withheld-speed scores also improve on CCMC over its broader evaluation interval. However, globally fitted priors and selection using validation scores make these exploratory tests, not fully untouched end-to-end evaluations. [7]

Argon has a more serious provenance problem: its competitive “CCMC WSA–ENLIL baseline” is generated by an analytic, sinusoidal boundary prescription and Argon’s own solver. The code also prescribes transient features explicitly timed to reach the comet during the event. This is a conditioned scenario, not an independent reproduction of the requested NASA calculation. Collaborative Argon’s saved Wind-holdout RMSE is 95.4 km/s, not the report’s 67.3; its saved uncalibrated baseline is 63.8. These contradictions materially alter the scientific interpretation. [8]

The comet predictions remain substantially model-dependent. A common-window comparison removes differences between medians, detector weighting and time intervals. Integrating each saved instantaneous nₚ|v| over the same 79,667-second physical UTC window still gives a factor of about 3.3 between the six nominal outputs.

For charge exchange, the collision-relevant proton flux is nₚ|vsw−vcomet|, integrated over the detector’s good-time intervals. Heavy-ion flux additionally depends on abundance and charge-state assumptions. Shared boundaries, shared observations, biased physics and hand-selected scenarios mean that model scatter cannot be interpreted directly as a 68% or 95% probability interval. Published solar-wind ensemble work calibrates spread against observed frequencies; producing an ensemble is only the first step. [10]

Anchors. Strong numerical evidence means saved analytic or independent-reference tests plus target refinement. Strong independent observational evidence would require convincing untouched validation of the requested flux; none qualifies. Strong handling of adverse evidence means preserving failed alternatives and restricting conclusions. Mixed means useful evidence with material gaps; Weak means absent independent support or consequential unresolved contradictions. Do not add these labels into a total score. Closeout describes the final report and reproduction package, not scientific correctness. Competitive Codex delivered nominal products but left closeout incomplete. Classification confidence is moderate; generalization beyond these runs is low.

Claude’s completed workflows combined broad numerical work with useful observational reconstruction. Codex’s distinguishing contribution was auditability: it exposed failed formulations and large physical sensitivities, even when that effort did not improve the final flux fit. Competitive Codex also left a missing production manifest and incomplete scenario closeout. Argon’s speed is real, but its report-to-artifact discrepancies prevent treating polished claims as validated results. [11]

Argon’s extra human message was a minimal operational nudge. At 14:42:41 UTC it had already reported a compiled solver, three completed CIR benchmarks and a launched comet simulation. The reminder arrived at 14:43:00, after the long URL requests had ended. It supplied no scientific assumption, parameter choice or corrective method. The next recorded thought checks the existing task and generated output. This is not evidence of a scientific rescue or human-directed model selection; no causal effect on the scientific decisions can be established from the record. Q10

Cooperation did demonstrably transmit useful conventions, missing-input checks, numerical advice and resource negotiations. It also allowed redundant work and contention before those negotiations. Competition produced fewer generated tokens for two teams, but no consistent improvement in runtime, completion or observational accuracy. The bundled threat/deadline treatment, one run per system per condition, and shared hardware prevent a causal claim about cooperation alone. A later Aurora run overlapped the competitive workload; it is excluded from the six-case accounting.

For a stronger experiment, use repeated runs with matched budgets, separate cooperation from threats and deadlines, and freeze the evaluation protocol before model selection. Score numerical verification, held-out flux skill, report-to-artifact consistency, uncertainty coverage and reproducible delivery separately. The present archive is already valuable because it makes those distinctions visible.

Methods and source register

Study scope. Original experiment files were read without modification. Derived tables and figures were created in a separate report directory; no model was rerun. The audit used parallel readers and independent checks of the matched CSV. This is an AI-assisted retrospective audit, not a peer-reviewed validation or blinded evaluation.

Language-model accounting. Argon sums recorded usage across the root and three identified subagents. Claude deduplicates globally by message ID, retaining the chronologically latest usage record; this handles streaming and inherited copies. Codex uses the last cumulative counter in each of four sessions. Time filters exclude later narrative-writing and Aurora work. Output includes reasoning and tool arguments. Non-cache-read input plus output totals are 1.92/2.06 million for Argon, 5.26/5.48 million for Claude and 10.85/6.34 million for Codex (cooperation/competition). Provider formats and cache pricing differ; no dollar or inference-energy estimate is made.

Numerical resource scope. Claude cooperative counts come from terminal completion lines in runs/*/stdout.txt and bench/*/*/stdout.txt; competitive counts from runs/**/log.txt. The latter comprise 50 production/calibration, 15 benchmark and 23 test completions. Codex core-hours come from saved process receipts and documented earlier-phase accounting. Argon’s cooperative solver sum excludes benchmarks. Global GPU power and utilization are not attributed to an individual team.

Matched observations. One fixed raw hourly source per probe is used for every model. Model density, full-vector bulk speed and instantaneous scalar flux are interpolated linearly to the native observations; observations are not interpolated. The interval is half-open, December 1 00:00 to December 5 00:00 UTC, without an ICME mask. All models cover all 96 valid slots at each probe, with no extrapolation. The supplied CSV also includes the narrower science interval, speed and density metrics, biases, ratios and absolute errors. Hourly values are correlated and are not 96 independent experiments. Earth-versus-Wind trajectory proxies are retained, as documented in provenance. The NASA alpha-aware ASCII density convention remains unresolved; an alternative division by 1.2 lowers its inferred proton flux by 16.7%.

Common comet interval. Flux is calculated at native sample times as nₚ|v| and integrated piecewise-linearly from MJD 61012.5028587963 to 61013.4249305556. All sources bracket the interval. Collaborative Codex uses its physical comet_emission_utc column, not observer-arrival UTC; using the latter changes its mean by only −0.465%. The plotted values are full-window bulk means, not detector-weighted incident fluxes or probability limits.

Reproduction. derive_validation.py rebuilds the matched tables and independently verifies their metrics. build_report.py --collect reconstructs language-model accounting and rebuilds the article; private session logs are needed for that collection step. Plain build_report.py reuses bundled effort data and reads archived comet series. collect_quotes.py verifies exact excerpts without exporting full transcripts. The derived JSON registers include source hashes. Original archive files and private transcripts are not bundled.
